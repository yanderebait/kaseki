# Terms of Use — Human Readable

## What is this?

Kaseki is a piece of software I wrote for myself in the evenings, between dinner and going to bed at an hour my younger self would have found embarrassing. I am giving it away because the only other thing I could do with it was let it rot on a drive until one of us — me or the drive — failed first, and the actuarial tables are not yet decisive on which. So here we are. You are reading the terms. I am imagined. Hello.

It runs on your computer. There is no server, no account, no cloud, no me, in any operationally meaningful sense, the moment your browser tab closes. I will become a name in a credits file you will never open. This is the correct amount of presence for a stranger who made you a free thing.

## Who's behind it?

Me. My name is yanderebait. I built this because I have lived most of my adult life renting software from companies that, if they noticed me at all, noticed me as a churn risk. I do not enjoy being a churn risk. I do not enjoy the email that arrives once a quarter informing me, with the warmth of a hospice nurse, that my price is going up. I do not enjoy logging in on a Tuesday morning to find that the feature I used every day has been moved behind a new tier called Pro+, and that the company is "excited" about it. They are excited because they have measured something. They are not excited the way you are excited when a friend has good news. They are excited the way a wolf is excited about a sheep, and they have an obligation to their shareholders to keep being excited for as long as the sheep is alive.

I am asking nothing of you, and I will be excited about nothing on a quarterly basis. This is, in 2026, almost a political position.

## What does it cost?

Nothing. Not now, not later, not on a special launch promotion. There is no subscription. There is no Pro tier. There is no Family Plan whose family-ness extends to four devices and ends abruptly on the fifth in the middle of an argument with your wife. There is no founder's edition with a numbered certificate. There is no Series A in our future during which I would otherwise have fired half the people who built Kaseki, replaced them with a chatbot named Luna, and announced the layoffs on LinkedIn with the words *we are doubling down on what matters most*. There is just Kaseki. It is mine. Now it is yours. The giving asks nothing in return except that you back up your own data, which I will get to in a moment, because it is the closest thing this document has to a moral, and you should not skip it the way you skip the safety briefing on planes.

## A note on owning things

There was a time, within living memory, when you could buy a thing and then own it. My father is old enough to remember this. He has books on a shelf that he bought in 1978, and they are, somehow, still his. The same is no longer true of most of what he buys now. The films he watches, the music he listens to, the photographs of his own grandchildren, the email archive containing forty years of his correspondence — all of it lives on servers owned by people he has never met, on terms that change at their convenience, and on the condition that he keeps paying, in money or in attention or in being marketed to, for the rest of his life. When he dies, none of it will pass to me. The books will. The Kindle library will be deleted within ninety days of the death certificate being processed, and Amazon will not consider this a tragedy, because Amazon does not consider anything a tragedy. They will consider it a routine account closure. There is a form.

If buying isn't owning, then piracy isn't stealing. And if piracy isn't stealing, then making the thing yourself — with your own hands, on a machine you own, from open source and stubbornness and an AI assistant that costs you twenty pounds a month — that isn't anywhere on the moral spectrum at all. That is just being a person. Kaseki is one such thing. I made it because the alternative was to keep paying rent forever on the inside of my own head, and I would like to die owning at least the contents of my own head, even if the head itself, by then, will have been somewhat reduced.

You don't have to live in the rented version of the world. The tools exist. The knowledge exists. AI fills in the gaps. Make what you want. Take what others made and gave away. Spend the money you would have given to some company on something a person made, or on dinner with somebody who still answers your texts, or on absolutely nothing, which is the most underrated category of spending in the developed world.

## What's the catch?

There isn't one, but there are three things you should understand before settling in, and they are arranged in increasing order of how much they will hurt.

The first is that I am probably not going to maintain this. I might. I probably won't. I built Kaseki because I needed it, and now I have it, and that need is satisfied, and software maintenance is to writing software what looking after the grave is to attending the funeral — same person, same place, none of the urgency, infinite quantities of it always waiting. If a bug ruins my day I will fix it. If a bug ruins your day, the source is right there on GitHub, and you are welcome to fix it yourself or wait, patiently, in the way one waits for a bus on a route that was cancelled in 2019.

The second is that there is no support. There is no help desk, no chat icon, no email address that reaches a human. If you find one and write to it, your message will arrive in an inbox where it will join three thousand other unread items and slowly drift down the screen, like a leaf falling, and we will both be slightly worse for the encounter. The code is in front of you. Read it. Don't.

The third is the one that matters. If you lose your data, it is gone. Not the soft gone of "ring customer services and they'll send a recovery link" — the hard gone. The kind that does not negotiate, that does not respond to grief or to money, that has the same finality as the moment in which your dog stops breathing in the vet's office and you understand, slightly too late, that you should have spent more of the past nine years on the floor with him. I do not have a copy. Nobody has a copy. The universe, in aggregate, is not interested in keeping a copy. Back up your data. Back it up to a second drive. Back that drive up to a third. Put one of those drives in a different building, and ideally a different relationship. The failure mode of doing nothing is total, and the day it happens you will remember reading this paragraph, and you will say, to nobody in particular, *he told me*.

You cannot take Kaseki, hide the source, and sell it as a closed-source service. That is the exact behaviour Kaseki was built in response to, and although the AGPL is enforced by the Free Software Foundation and not by me, if you do it I will know, and I will think less of you, and you will not be able to feel it, but it will be true, and on a long enough timeline the universe arranges itself around people thinking less of you, and you will wonder, late in life, why things did not go better.

## Can I sue you?

Not productively. Kaseki is provided as-is, with no warranty of any kind, expressed or implied or whispered in the dark. If it eats your data, deletes the only digital copy of the letter you wrote to your father and never sent, corrupts the manuscript you have been working on for six years and have not yet shown anyone because you are afraid they will be right, or loses the encrypted folder containing the things about yourself that you do not, even alone, want to be reminded you have done — that is genuinely regrettable, and you have my sympathies, and it is not actionable. The deal with free software made by one person and given away in exchange for nothing is that the person making it is not on the hook for what happens after.

If you wanted somebody on the hook, you should have bought enterprise software, which would have failed in roughly the same way and on roughly the same Tuesday morning, but you would have had a customer success manager named Brendan to absorb your distress, and Brendan, by the end of the call, would have been the second-saddest person on the line.

## Can I be sued for what I do with Kaseki?

Possibly, but not by me. Most of you have a job. Most jobs come with a regulator — a body whose website was designed in 2008 and last updated in 2014, staffed by a compliance officer who imagines, every morning, the worst thing the people in their profession could be doing, writes a memo about it, goes home, has dinner, and dies, eventually, having spent forty years thinking the worst of strangers for a salary that did not, in real terms, keep up with inflation. Whatever rules your regulator has imposed on you continue to apply when you do your work inside Kaseki. I have not lobbied any of them on your behalf. If your professional body requires client data to be stored in a particular way, that requirement holds, and how you satisfy it is between you and them and the compliance officer whose name you will never learn.

## What happens if you stop?

I will, eventually. Either by deciding I have had enough — most likely — or by being run over by a cyclist on Euston Road, the kind of cyclist who runs over pedestrians and feels morally vindicated about it, and who will not stop. Either way, nothing changes for you. The code is yours. It runs on your computer. It will continue to run on your computer for as long as your computer runs, faithfully, in the way of a dog that has not yet been told the owner is not coming home, and that will spend the rest of its life sitting by the door, looking up hopefully every time the wind moves the letterbox, until one day it stops sitting there, because something in it has worked out what nobody is telling it. The licence permits you to keep developing Kaseki. The licence permits you to fork it and call it Hozuki or Senku or whatever Dr Stone reference is most painful to me posthumously. The only thing that ends when I end is my involvement, which, retrospectively, will turn out to have been smaller than either of us was pretending.

## Whose courts?

If you have a dispute with me about Kaseki that cannot be resolved by closing the program and going outside for fifteen minutes, it will be resolved in the courts of England and Wales, which are slow, expensive, and statistically unlikely to be enthusiastic about your being there, or mine.

## Do these terms change?

They might. If they do, the new version ships in the next release, and you will see it the next time you update. There is no mailing list because I do not have your email and would not write to you even if I did. Read the new version, or don't. # Privacy Policy — Human Readable

## What does Kaseki do with my data?

Nothing. It writes your data to your computer, encrypts it, and then forgets about it the way a man forgets about a parent he has not phoned in two months — without intent, without ceremony, just the steady drift of attention away from what was meant to matter. That is the entire feature. This is, as far as I can determine, the most aggressively pro-user privacy architecture currently legal in the United Kingdom, which is a sentence that flatters Kaseki considerably less than it indicts everyone else.

## Can you, the developer, see my notes?

No. This is not a promise. A promise is what you receive from a company that technically can read your notes and is asking you, sweetly, to believe that they won't. What Kaseki offers is an architectural fact, which is structurally stronger than a promise, because it does not depend on my continuing good behaviour, or my continuing employment in this project, or my continuing to be alive. The server does not exist. The endpoint does not exist. The cloud does not exist. Your computer never transmits anything to me, because there is no me in the relevant sense — there is a string of letters in a credits file you will not read, and an address in north-west London that does not, in any operational way, know who you are.

If I genuinely wanted to read your notes I would have to break into your house, which is a lot of effort for a man whose evenings are dedicated to making tea and being asleep by ten, and even if I made it past the door I would not know which laptop was yours, and by the time I worked it out the dog or the neighbour or the woman in your life would have noticed, and we would all, the dog included, have a worse evening than we had been planning.

## What about Microsoft, Google, Apple, Meta, Amazon?

I cannot stop your operating system from spying on you. This is not Kaseki's failing. This is the consequence of thirty years of choices, made mostly by men in California, to build the entire architecture of modern life on a single load-bearing premise: that human attention can be packaged and sold, and that the person whose attention it was does not need to be consulted about the arrangement. We all signed the terms. We all clicked Accept. The terms were sixty pages long, written by lawyers paid more in a year than most of us will see in a decade, and the clicking was the consent, and the consent is, in legal terms, binding. We did this to ourselves. Or rather, we did it to each other, and the people who built the apparatus did it to us, and we cannot quite agree, as a species, on how much of the responsibility is ours.

Here is the state of play as I understand it.

Windows is, structurally, a surveillance device with a word processor bolted on. Microsoft recently shipped a feature called Recall that screenshots your computer every few seconds and feeds the images into a model, presented to the public as a productivity innovation and almost certainly described, in internal documents, as a category-defining new advertising substrate. The press objected. Microsoft walked it back. It is coming back, because the people whose job it was to not ship it have been quietly reassigned to other teams, and the fortune sitting on the table has not moved.

Apple is the same arrangement in a kinder typeface. They sell you the story that they are the privacy-respecting one, and to be fair they have built some of the architecture to deliver on a meaningful fraction of it — and then they tried to scan every photograph you took on the way to iCloud, against a database, and walked that back too, and that is coming back as well, for the same reason.

Google has spent twenty years and several hundred billion pounds assembling the most comprehensive psychological profile of human behaviour in the history of our species, and is using it, today, in the year of our lord 2026, to recommend trainers to you that you bought last week.

Meta has done the same thing for your relationships, which is somehow worse, because the relationships are the only things any of us actually have. The algorithm has spent a decade quietly optimising itself for the precise emotional state that gets you to click on one more story, which has turned out, in millions of teenage girls worldwide, to be despair. The internal research showed this. The internal research was buried. The senior people read it, and went home, and had dinner with their own children, and put their own phones in another room, and went to sleep. They knew. They know.

Amazon has done it for the inside of your house, and for what you watch on the television in your living room, and for what you ask the small plastic cylinder on the counter when you think nobody is listening — except the small plastic cylinder is listening, and so is the team that maintains it, and so is an unpaid contractor in Ohio rating the transcripts for accuracy, and so, on a sufficiently bad afternoon, is somebody who is bored.

I cannot stop any of this. Neither can you. None of us can. The most we can do, individually, is make our own small corner of the situation a little less complicit. Kaseki is my corner. I do not share data with Microsoft. I do not share data with anyone. I have no data to share. I do not know who you are. I do not know that you exist. If somebody asked me, under oath, in a courtroom, how many people use Kaseki, I would have to answer honestly: I assume three. I assume two of them are you, on different machines, and the third is a researcher writing a paper about people like you.

## What if the government asks?

They can ask. I have nothing to give them. A subpoena served on me would be returned with the note that the recipient does not possess the requested records and does not, in any meaningful sense, know who you are. This is not heroism. Heroism is producible on demand and requires the producer to be brave on a particular day. What Kaseki offers is a structural inability to produce records that were never collected, which is the most reliable form of resistance available to a small developer in a flat in London, because it does not depend on his being brave on a day when he is, frankly, going to be tired.

If a government genuinely wants your data, they will need to come to your house and take the computer. That is between you and them, and depending on which government we are talking about and what you have been writing down, it may be a real and frightening problem, and one Kaseki cannot help you with. Encryption helps a little. Living somewhere that respects due process helps more. Being uninteresting enough that nobody wants your data in the first place is, in cold statistical terms, the most effective protection most of us have access to, and I would gently invite you to consider whether your insistence on being interesting is paying the dividends you think it is.

## What happens when I delete something?

It enters a ten-second undo window, and then it is gone. There is no archive. There is no recycle bin on a server in Frankfurt. Nobody has a copy. Nobody can get you one. Delete with intent, and accept that some of what you delete you will, on a particular Thursday three weeks from now, find yourself wishing you had not — which is a feature, not a bug. The squirrels do not keep everything. The squirrels bury the nuts and then forget where most of them are, and the forgotten nuts become trees, and this is how forests work. Forgetting is part of the system. We are the only species that has tried to opt out, and the data on how well it is going for us is now coming in, and it is not flattering.

## What about the optional network features?

Three. Two are off by default. The update check is on by default. All disablable.

The update check, unless you switch it off, asks GitHub once each time you start Kaseki whether a newer version exists; the request contains your version number and nothing else. If there is one, Kaseki asks you first, and downloads it from GitHub only if you say yes. The weather widget, if you place one on your dashboard, asks a weather provider for the forecast at the location you have configured — a city name or coordinates, no more — which means that an organisation in Switzerland now knows that somebody in your general area would like to know whether to bring a coat, and is welcome to do with that information whatever they like. The crash reporter, if you opt in, sends a stack trace when Kaseki crashes — function names, line numbers, the kind of cryptic technical incantation that means something to me and nothing to you, with no tasks or notes or files attached.

If all three are off, Kaseki makes zero network connections, ever, and you can verify this with any network monitor, which I encourage you to do — not because I am lying, but because you should not take anyone's word on this, including mine, and certainly including the word of the rather larger companies on whose word you have, until now, been content to rely.

## Cookies, trackers, fingerprinting?

None. No website to put cookies on. No analytics measuring how long you hover over the Delete button before pressing it. No library cataloguing the rhythm of your keystrokes to identify you across sessions — which is a real thing, deployed against you, today, on at least six of the tabs in the browser you are using to read this. The industry built around these techniques is one of the most morally bankrupt enterprises currently operating at scale within the law, and I would happily watch all of it burn, but in the meantime I have to respect the hustle, in the way one respects the hustle of a man who has built a profitable career out of selling kidneys he did not buy.

## Children?

Kaseki is not aimed at children. It also collects nothing from anyone, so if a child uses it the privacy implications are identical to those of an adult, which is to say none. If you are a child and you have read this far down the page, I am genuinely a little impressed; the privacy policies of most companies that profit from you are written precisely so that you will not get this far. Close the laptop. Go outside. The internet will still be there when you get back, and it will be worse, because you will be older, and the people running it will have had another five years to think about how to monetise the loneliness they spent the previous five years giving you.

## What are my rights under UK GDPR?

You have the right to access your data, correct it, delete it, restrict its processing, object to its processing, and port it elsewhere. In Kaseki these rights are exercised by, in order: opening Kaseki, editing things, deleting things, not doing things, not doing things, and clicking the Export button. There is no form. There is no thirty-day waiting period. There is no compliance officer named Geoffrey who will respond from a queue of eight thousand identical requests filed by people who have realised, somewhere around the age of forty-two, what was done to them. The data is on your computer. You are the data controller. You have always had these rights — what UK GDPR did was force everybody else to grudgingly admit that you had them too, and most of them are still in court about it, and will be when you are dead. Kaseki gives them to you by default, because there is no architecturally possible way to deny them, which is roughly the lowest bar a software company can clear, and almost nobody else is managing it.

## Does this policy change?

It might. If it does, the new version ships in the next release. If a future version of Kaseki ever does collect anything — which it will not, but if — you will be told in plain English what is collected and given the chance to refuse. If a version of Kaseki ever appears in the wild that collects data without telling you, it is not Kaseki. It is something wearing Kaseki's clothes, walking around the kitchen at night, hoping you will not notice the way it moves.

# Acceptable Use Policy — Human Readable

## Can you actually enforce any of this?

No. Kaseki runs on your computer. I have no visibility into what you do with it, no record that you exist, and no technical means of intervening in your installation even if I wanted to. This document is not a list of rules I will catch you breaking. It is a list of things I would prefer you didn't do, written in the knowledge that, for most of you, telling you is enough — and that, for the rest of you, nothing was ever going to be enough, and the police will, in time, attend to it.

## What shouldn't I use Kaseki for?

The serious ones first. Without jokes. Some things should not have jokes around them.

Do not use Kaseki to plan or coordinate harm against another person. Surveillance of a partner. Stalking. Building a dossier on a private individual you have decided to be obsessed with — and you know whether you are doing this, even if you have told yourself a different word for it. Do not use Kaseki to store material whose existence is a crime, of which child sexual abuse material is the obvious example, and there are others you can work out for yourself. Do not use it to coordinate violence, or fraud against people who cannot afford to lose, or anything in that family. The fact that Kaseki is encrypted and local does not make it a sanctuary for any of this. It makes it a place where, when the police arrive, the evidence is sitting on the same device they have come to collect, and you will have arranged for them to do all of their work in one trip. If you are doing any of the above, the problem is not which task manager you are using. The problem is upstream of every piece of software ever written, and there is nothing in this document, and frankly nothing in any document, that is going to reach it.

That is the serious section. Everything below this point is opinions of mine, which you are welcome to disagree with, and which you will mostly recognise, because I am describing you.

## Productivity pornography

You agree not to use Kaseki to maintain the appearance of productivity at the expense of the fact of it. This includes, but is not limited to: creating a task for something you have already done so you can immediately tick it off and feel briefly, fraudulently powerful; subdividing a single task into eleven subtasks because the original task is frightening and the subtasks are small enough to be performed by the version of you that exists today; and taking a screenshot of your dashboard to post on a platform owned by a man whose entire personality is being good at this kind of thing, in the hope that strangers will recognise, in your task list, the version of yourself you have not yet become.

We have all done it. I have done it this week. The trick is not to stop, which is impossible, but to notice when you are doing it, and to feel a small specific shame, and to let that shame perform its quiet work on you, the way a tide reshapes a coastline over decades, except that you do not have decades.

## The customisation trap

You agree not to spend more than thirty minutes in a single sitting customising your Kaseki workspace. Choosing icons. Renaming spaces. Adjusting the colour of tags. Reordering columns. Tweaking the dashboard layout. None of this is work. All of it is work-shaped activity that the brain accepts as a substitute for work, and the brain is doing this on purpose, because the brain is afraid, and the work it is afraid of is the work where you would have to confront the question of whether you have spent your life on the right things. The colour of the Projects tab is a much safer question. You have spent your whole life on safer questions. You are running out of life.

I know this because I have done it. I am, in some quite specific sense, still doing it — Kaseki itself is, on a long enough timeline, the most elaborate version of this anyone has ever attempted — and the only honest thing I can do at this point is warn you.

## Tasks that are not tasks

You agree not to create tasks that you know, in the small honest hour of the morning when your eyes are open and the room is dark and you are not yet sure whether you have woken or are still asleep, you are never going to do. You know the ones. Learn French. Start running. Read Ulysses. Call Mum more often. Become the kind of person who has a morning routine. Meditate. Journal every day. Take up the violin again. Write the book. Reach out to that friend I haven't spoken to since 2019. Sort out my will. Speak to my father about the thing.

These are not tasks. These are confessions. They are indictments of how you are currently living, dressed up as tasks so that you can keep them visible without having to act on them — the way some people keep a treadmill in the spare bedroom as a coathanger that occasionally also generates guilt, the way some people keep a dead parent's number in their phone for nine years after the funeral because deleting it feels like a second death.

Either do them or delete them. Carrying them around indefinitely is doing slow violence to your self-image, and you have not consciously noticed, but the part of you keeping score has, and that part is the one that decides whether you sleep, and how, and what dreams arrive.

I will gently raise the possibility that *Call Mum more often*, in particular, has a closing window, the size of which neither of us can see, and that some of you reading this will, within ten years, have lost the ability to act on that task no matter how often you tick it back into the To Do column. I am sorry to be the one to say it. Somebody had to.

## The 3am notes problem

You agree not to type your feelings into a task description at three in the morning.

I know that the Notes feature is, technically, the correct place for this. I built it. I know what it is for. And yet — if you find yourself there, in the dark, the laptop a small pale rectangle in an otherwise dark room, writing a personal manifesto about productivity or dietary restrictions or cryptocurrency or your career or your country or a woman who has stopped replying to your messages — close the laptop.

Just close it. Put it on the floor. Text someone who loves you. If nobody loves you, that is, regrettably, outside the scope of this Acceptable Use Policy and is, broadly speaking, the human condition, but the answer is still not to write more in the task description. The answer is never to write more in the task description. The task description does not love you back. The task description, given enough of you, will start to resemble you, and that is a worse mirror than any other mirror in the house.

If you are in a place where the laptop at 3am is the safest thing you can think of to do, please call somebody. The Samaritans in the UK are 116 123, free, twenty-four hours, and will not judge you. They are better at this than your task manager. They are better at this than me. I do not know what is happening to you, but I know the next move, and the next move is not in the task description.

## Workspace hygiene

You agree to archive completed tasks with some regularity. A Kaseki workspace containing 847 completed tasks, all of them ticked, all of them still on the screen, is a small war crime against your future self and a clear visual signal to anyone who glances at your laptop in a café that something has gone slightly wrong in your interior life — and they will not say anything, because they are British, but they will think it, and they will think it kindly, and the kindness will hurt more than the judgement would have.

The completed tasks are not trophies. They are not a record of who you were. They are receipts, and what one does with receipts, eventually, is throw them away.

## AI-generated content

You may use AI tools to draft a task description, summarise a note, or rephrase something you wrote badly. Kaseki itself uses AI in places, and I am in no position to be precious about it.

What you may not do is have an AI generate your daily task list at six in the morning and close the loop on it at midnight without your meaningful participation. At that point you have automated yourself out of your own life, which is on offer from several companies today as a paid feature, and which represents a form of functional death that no productivity software can help you with. Your great-grandfather, who fought in a war you have read about briefly, would have a question about this. I will not put words in his mouth. You know what the question is.

## Meal planning

You agree not to engage in serious meal planning for more than three consecutive days. Longitudinal studies — I am inventing the studies, but the finding is correct — have established that three days is the threshold beyond which a person begins, almost imperceptibly, to become the kind of person who owns a spice rack with the spices labelled. From there the slope is steep, and the destination is grim, and from the bottom of the slope you cannot see the top, and the people on the way down do not believe they are on the way down. We are trying to help you.

## The timer

You agree not to start the time tracker, get distracted for forty minutes by a YouTube essay about a video game you have never played, remember the timer is running, and then lie to yourself about what percentage of those forty minutes was productive. The timer knows. You know. I know. I built the timer and I have done the same thing to it and felt the same small shame.

Log the honest number. The whole point of measuring something is that the measurement is true. A measurement that flatters you is not a measurement. It is a small lie, and the more small lies you tell yourself the further the version of yourself in your head drifts from the version of yourself sitting in the chair, and that drift, accumulated over years, is responsible for a non-trivial fraction of the unhappiness in the developed world. The honest number is, in most cases, less interesting than the lie. It is also, in most cases, where the real life is.

## Naming things

Any user who names a space *Stuff* or *Misc* or *Other* hereby waives the right to complain that they cannot find anything, and accepts that the problem is not a software problem but a deeper one, located somewhere between executive function and the willingness to admit that the objects in your life have categories, and that you have been refusing to assign them because assigning them would commit you to a version of yourself you have not yet agreed to be.

## What if my job has rules about what software I can use?

It probably does. Solicitors have the SRA. Doctors have the GMC. Accountants have the ICAEW. Therapists have the BACP. Architects have ARB. Financial advisors have the FCA. Veterinarians have the RCVS. Teachers have the TRA. Pharmacists have the GPhC. Pilots have the CAA. Nurses have the NMC. There are roughly forty more for the rest of you, all with three-letter acronyms, all with websites designed in 2008, all run by a quiet British committee whose members have largely forgotten the work the regulations were originally meant to govern, and whose meetings are minuted in a font that none of you will ever see.

Whatever rules apply to your profession when you do your work, they continue to apply when you do your work inside Kaseki. I have not negotiated an exemption on your behalf with any of them. I have not tried. If your regulator requires client data to be stored in a particular way, that requirement holds, and how you satisfy it is between you and them. Kaseki is a piece of software, not a legal instrument, and does not absorb your professional obligations or modify them in any direction.

## What about encryption laws?

Some countries restrict the use, import, or export of strong encryption. Kaseki uses SQLCipher to encrypt the sensitive folder, which qualifies. If you live somewhere this is restricted, that is between you and your government, and is probably one of the smaller questions you should be asking about your living situation — the larger ones being why the encryption is restricted in the first place, and what else, by extension, is being restricted in the same direction. I do not maintain a list of jurisdictions where Kaseki may legally be used. The working-out is your responsibility, and if the working-out concludes that you cannot legally use Kaseki where you live, the broader implications of that finding are probably going to occupy you for a while.

## What will you do if you find out I am misusing Kaseki?

Almost nothing. I cannot revoke your access — you have the code. I cannot disable your installation — it runs on a machine I have no relationship with. I cannot identify you — I have no record of you. What I can do is decline to merge your contributions, decline to provide informal help if you ever ask, and decline to produce official releases on your behalf, none of which will prevent you from continuing to use the copy of Kaseki you already have, and all of which will be administered by me in my own time, without due process, without an appeal mechanism, and according to whichever mood I am in.

The honest position is that this document is a statement of values, not a contract I can enforce. Kaseki is in the world now. What it gets used for is up to the people who have it. I am asking you, as one person who has read this far to another, to use it for things that do not hurt anyone, and that ideally do not hurt you either — because the long quiet erosion of self that comes from spending years lying to your own task manager is, in aggregate, more damaging than most of what people worry about, and the people in your life are going to need you intact for what comes next.

Take care of yourself. Back up your data. Phone your mother.
